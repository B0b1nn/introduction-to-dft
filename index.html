DFT lecture notes, Part 2

Part 1 derived the Kohn-Sham equations and the SCF cycle. This part covers what is needed to turn them into real predictions: forces and equilibrium structures, the choice of exchange-correlation functional, pseudopotentials, and the most notorious failure of standard DFT, the band gap problem.

1. Forces and equilibrium structures

Part 1 held the nuclei fixed. To predict a structure we must let them move. Start from the full equation including nuclear kinetic energy −∑∇²I/2MI and nuclear repulsion. Because nuclei are much heavier than electrons, they stay close to their equilibrium positions while the electrons move around them. This is the adiabatic (Born-Oppenheimer) approximation: for each set of nuclear positions the electrons sit in their ground state, whose energy is E(R₁,…,RM).

The nuclei then move on an effective potential energy surface:

U(R₁,…,RM) = ½ ∑I≠J ZIZJ/|RI − RJ| + E(R₁,…,RM)

The nuclear wavefunction is far more compact than the electronic one, so for equilibrium structures (except for the lightest elements such as hydrogen) the nuclei can be treated as classical point particles. Replacing the nuclear momentum by its classical counterpart gives Newton's law:

MI d²RI/dt² = −∂U/∂RI = FI

The force on a nucleus can be evaluated with classical electrostatics: it is the Coulomb force exerted on that nucleus by the other nuclei and by the electron density n(r) (the Hellmann-Feynman, or electrostatic, theorem). So one SCF calculation gives both the energy and all forces.

Finding the structure

The equilibrium structure is where the net force on every nucleus vanishes, FI = 0. In practice one runs an SCF calculation, evaluates the forces, moves the atoms downhill in energy, and repeats until the forces are below a threshold. Cell shape and volume can be optimised too. This is why DFT can predict lattice parameters and bond lengths without experimental input.

2. Choosing an exchange-correlation functional

The Hohenberg-Kohn theorem guarantees that an exact functional exists but does not say what it is. Every practical calculation therefore uses an approximation, and results depend on the choice, so the functional used must always be stated (not just "a DFT calculation").

LevelIngredientsComments
LDALocal density n(r) only; each volume element treated as a homogeneous electron gas.Simple and surprisingly good for structures. Its Vxc is a continuous function of density (relevant to band gaps, Section 4).
GGAn(r) and its gradient |∇n|.Many different GGAs exist because the gradient can be included in many ways. Widely used in solids: PW91 and PBE. More information does not guarantee higher accuracy.
Beyond GGAAdditional physical information.A hierarchy of functionals gradually adds more detail; treat these as refinements, with higher cost.

Two corrections that sit on top of Kohn-Sham DFT are worth knowing by name: GW quasiparticle corrections for band gaps, and dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) for Mott-Hubbard insulators with localised d or f electrons. Both start from the Kohn-Sham eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.

3. Plane waves, cutoffs and pseudopotentials

Periodic solids are modelled with a supercell. By Bloch's theorem each solution is a plane wave times a function with the cell's periodicity, and the periodic part is expanded in plane waves exp[iG·r]. The expansion is truncated at a cutoff energy Ecut = ħ²G²cut/2m, which must be converged.

The difficulty is the core electrons. Tightly bound core states oscillate on very short length scales and would need a very large cutoff. But bonding and most material properties are governed by the valence electrons. A pseudopotential replaces the core electrons by a smooth effective potential, so only the valence electrons are treated explicitly. This is the frozen core approximation; calculations without it are called all-electron and are used far less widely.

  • Transferability: a pseudopotential made from an isolated atom should work in any chemical environment without adjustment. DFT codes ship libraries with one for most elements.
  • Hard vs soft: a hard pseudopotential needs a high cutoff, a soft one a low cutoff. Vanderbilt's ultrasoft pseudopotentials (USPPs) need substantially lower cutoffs, at the price of more empirical construction parameters.
  • Each pseudopotential defines a minimum cutoff for the atoms it describes. When energies of different systems are compared (for example fcc Pd, fcc Cu and a bcc CuPd alloy), use the same, highest required cutoff in all of them.

4. Band structures and the band gap problem

The Kohn-Sham eigenvalues εᵢ(k) form the band structure. In a metal the next available state lies infinitesimally above the highest occupied one; in a semiconductor or insulator there is a finite energy difference, the band gap. In Kohn-Sham theory:

EgKS = εCBB − εVBT   (conduction-band bottom minus valence-band top)

Experiments measure something different: the quasiparticle (electrical) gap, built from total energies of the neutral system and of the system with one electron removed or added (EN−1, EN, EN+1):

Egqp = (EN−1 − EN) − (EN − EN+1) = IP − EA

The first bracket is the ionisation potential (work function in a solid), the second the electron affinity. For a small molecule one can compute this directly from three total-energy calculations (the ΔSCF method). In a solid, adding one electron to about 10²⁰ changes the density by a negligible amount, and taking that limit gives:

Egqp = EgKS + Δxc ,   Δxc = lim [Vxc(n + Δn) − Vxc(n − Δn)]

With the exact functional, Vxc jumps discontinuously when an electron is added, so Δxc ≠ 0. LDA, by construction, has a continuous Vxc, so Δxc = 0 and the gap cannot come out right. As a rule of thumb, Kohn-Sham gaps underestimate measured ones by about 40%; errors above 1 eV are common. GW quasiparticle calculations built on the Kohn-Sham solution fix most of this.

Practical consequence: standard DFT should not be the final judge when exact absorption or emission thresholds matter (LEDs, solar cells, photodiodes); post-DFT corrections are needed there. Also, for optical spectra the electronic excitations are outside ground-state DFT altogether.

5. Checklist for a trustworthy calculation

  1. Choose and state the functional (LDA, PBE, ...).
  2. Converge k points before a series of calculations; report them. Compare supercells at comparable k-point density.
  3. Converge Ecut; use one common cutoff when comparing energies.
  4. Check the pseudopotentials are appropriate for the elements and the property.
  5. Make sure the SCF cycle converges to a tight tolerance, then relax until forces are small.
  6. Ask whether the property is a ground-state property. If it is a band gap, an excited-state, van der Waals or strongly correlated d/f problem, expect a systematic error and consider GW, DFT+U/DMFT or dispersion corrections.
