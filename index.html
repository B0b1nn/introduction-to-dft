Materials modelling with density functional theory

Density functional theory (DFT) makes it possible to compute the electronic structure and many properties of materials from the laws of quantum mechanics and the atomic numbers alone. These notes follow the development of the theory from the many-body Schrödinger equation to the Kohn-Sham scheme, and then to practical calculations and applications.

Introduction: modelling from first principles

Materials modelling is the use of mathematical models to describe and predict material properties quantitatively. A model is said to be from first principles (ab initio) when it uses no empirical input: only the Schrödinger equation, the periodic table and a computer. This contrasts with top-down approaches, in which parameters are fitted to experimental data.

At the atomic scale, a material is a set of electrons and nuclei interacting through the Coulomb force. Reasons for the popularity of DFT include its transferability (one method for molecules, solids, surfaces and interfaces), the simple single-particle picture it yields, and predictions that match many measurements to within a few percent.

Typical successes discussed in Giustino's text include the structure of detonation nanodiamonds ("bucky diamonds"), the heat capacity of MgB₂ (calculated Tc = 39.4 K against the measured 39 K), a lower bound of 6,670 K for the temperature at the Earth's inner-core boundary, and crack propagation in a 174,752-atom silicon model.

The many-body problem and the exponential wall

In Hartree atomic units (ħ = mₑ = e = 4πε₀ = 1; 1 bohr = 0.529177 Å, 1 Ha = 27.2114 eV), and with nuclei held fixed because Mᵢ ≥ 1836 mₑ (clamped-nuclei approximation), the electronic problem reads:

[ −½ ∑ ∇ᵢ² + ∑ Vₙ(rᵢ) + ½ ∑ 1/|rᵢ − rⱼ| ] Ψ(r₁,…,r_N) = E Ψ

Discretising space on a uniform mesh, the wavefunction of N electrons and M nuclei requires NpN+M complex numbers. For the silicon unit cell (volume a³/4 with a = 5.43 Å) a spacing of about 0.1 Å gives Np ≈ 40,000 points; with 8 valence electrons and 2 nuclei (10 particles) this amounts to about 10⁴⁶ numbers. The cost grows exponentially with system size, hence the name exponential wall.

Single-particle approximations

Independent electrons
Neglect electron-electron repulsion: Ψ = φ₁φ₂…φ_N and E = ∑εᵢ.
Limitation: the Coulomb repulsion is ignored entirely.
Exclusion principle
Electrons are identical fermions, so Ψ must change sign when two electrons are exchanged. A Slater determinant of the φᵢ enforces this; the density is n(r) = ∑|φᵢ(r)|².
Mean field (Hartree)
Each electron moves in the average electrostatic potential of the others, VH(r) = ∫ n(r′)/|r − r′| dr′, equivalent to ∇²VH = −4πn.
Limitation: no exchange and no correlation.
Hartree-Fock
Minimising the energy of a Slater determinant adds the Fock exchange potential VX(r, r′) = −∑ⱼ φⱼ*(r′)φⱼ(r)/|r − r′|, which is non-local.
Limitation: electron correlation is still missing.
Kohn-Sham
Work with the density instead of the wavefunction; see the next two sections.

The Hohenberg-Kohn theorem

The ground-state energy is a functional of the density alone, E = F[n]. Although Ψ depends on 3N variables, n(r) depends on only three. The result follows from three premises:

ndeterminesVₙdeterminesΨdeterminesE

The second and third links are straightforward. The first, n → Vₙ, is proved by reductio ad absurdum. Suppose two potentials Vₙ ≠ Vₙ′ gave the same ground-state density n, with ground-state energies E and E′. Since Ψ is not the ground state of Vₙ′, and vice versa:

E + ∫ n (Vₙ′ − Vₙ) dr > E′   and   E′ + ∫ n (Vₙ − Vₙ′) dr > E

Adding the two inequalities gives E + E′ > E + E′, a contradiction. Hence the density fixes the external potential uniquely. A second statement, the variational principle, says the true density minimises E[n], i.e. δE/δn = 0 at n₀. For excited states the full wavefunction is still needed.

Remark. A functional maps a function to a number (a definite integral is the simplest example). The functional derivative δE/δn measures how E responds to a small local change in the whole function n(r).

Kohn-Sham equations, LDA and self-consistency

Kohn and Sham mapped the interacting system onto a fictitious non-interacting one with the same ground-state density. All unknown many-body effects are collected in the exchange-correlation functional Exc[n]:

[ −½∇² + Vₙ(r) + VH(r) + Vxc(r) ] φᵢ(r) = εᵢ φᵢ(r)  ,  Vxc = δExc/δn

The exact Exc is unknown. In the local density approximation (LDA) each volume element dr contributes the exchange-correlation energy of a homogeneous electron gas (HEG) at the local density n(r). The exchange part is analytic, Ex = −¾(3/π)1/3 ∫ n4/3 dr, giving Vx = −(3/π)1/3 n1/3. The correlation part is parametrised from the quantum Monte Carlo data of Ceperley and Alder (Perdew-Zunger form, in terms of the Wigner-Seitz radius rs). For densities typical of semiconductors the correlation energy is about an order of magnitude smaller than the exchange energy.

Self-consistent field (SCF) cycle

VH and Vxc depend on n, and n depends on the φᵢ, so the equations must be solved self-consistently:

  1. Fix the nuclear positions, compute Vₙ, and guess n(r), for example by superposing isolated-atom densities.
  2. Compute VH (Poisson equation) and Vxc; form Vtot = Vₙ + VH + Vxc.
  3. Solve the Kohn-Sham eigenvalue problem for φᵢ and εᵢ.
  4. Build the new density nnew = ∑|φᵢ|².
  5. If nnew and nold agree within the tolerance (in practice, often the total energies of successive iterations are compared), stop. Otherwise mix the densities and return to step 2.

Practical calculations (Sholl and Steckel)

Real calculations on crystals use a periodic supercell. By Bloch's theorem the solutions have the form φk(r) = exp(ik·r) uk(r), where uk has the periodicity of the cell and is expanded in plane waves. Two numerical parameters follow, and both must be converged:

ParameterMeaningGood practice
k pointsSampling of the Brillouin zone for integrals in reciprocal space.Test convergence before a series of calculations; report the k points used. Larger supercells need fewer. Metals need special treatment.
Cutoff energyThe plane-wave sum is truncated at Ecut = ħ²G²cut/2m.Report Ecut. When comparing energies of different systems, use the same cutoff in all calculations (the largest one required).

Finding an equilibrium structure means iterating until the forces on the nuclei vanish. The nuclei are treated as classical point particles, and the force on nucleus I is FI = −∂U/∂RI.

Application: computational screening of catalysts

Hydrogen can be produced by the hydrogen evolution reaction, with platinum the best catalyst but also very rare and expensive. Greeley et al. (2006) searched for alternatives using a descriptor: the Gibbs energy of hydrogen adsorption ΔGH. Following Parsons, activity is maximised when |ΔGH| is close to zero; for Pt, ΔGH = −0.1 eV.

Stability was assessed through Gibbs energies of degradation processes (solute segregation, oxidation, corrosion). The alloy BiPt was then synthesised, and its measured catalytic activity exceeded that of pure Pt, as predicted. The lesson is that DFT can screen even hypothetical materials before synthesis.

Scope and limitations

DFT addresses the electronic ground state. Because the exact functional is unknown, practical results carry an intrinsic uncertainty that is often assessed only by comparison with experiment.

Calculated reliablyProblematic cases
Equilibrium structures and lattice parametersBand gaps of semiconductors and insulators are underestimated; errors above 1 eV are common, and even the exact Kohn-Sham functional has this problem. Remedy: quasiparticle (GW) corrections.
Binding energies and cohesive energiesMott-Hubbard insulators with localised d or f states. Remedy: dynamical mean-field theory.
Vibrations and vibrational spectravan der Waals forces arise from long-range correlation and are poorly described by standard functionals (sparse matter, proteins).
Ionisation potentials, electron affinities, band structures of metals and semiconductorsExcited states: the Hohenberg-Kohn theorems apply to the ground state only.

Cost is a further limit: calculations with tens of atoms are routine, hundreds of atoms are challenging, and a thousand or more are restricted to specialised groups with very large computers. Even where DFT is inaccurate, the Kohn-Sham eigenvalues and eigenfunctions remain a useful starting point for more refined methods.
